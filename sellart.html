How to Turn a Profit as a Struggling Artist

While I was putting myself through university, I paid for my day-to-day needs by, in large part, taking the initiative and looking for the simplest ways to spin up a bit of money through some of my hobbies. For a long time now, I've liked to think of myself as artsy. I enjoy simply engaging with the creative process for its own sake. But, given certain economic realities, it also doesn't necessarily hurt to think about how to monetise your creative output.

I'm by no means some big name commercial success when it comes to art. I'm not commanding six-figure bidding sprees at gallery auctions, and I doubt I will any time soon. And these days, I do earn my living through much more conventional employment. But for quite some time, I was very profitably, and very happily, making creative material to sell independently.

And here I want to share the practical knowledge of how to do that sort of thing effectively. That means: how to approach the logistics of it all. What few basic resources you'll need to start. Exactly how to make all sorts of material that will sell. And perhaps most crucially, how to do all this at the lowest possible cost, so you can actually make some decent money for the time you put into it.

By the time I started selling art, I had already done a bit of painting and sculpting and the like here and there - explored different artistic mediums - all just for fun. Creating stuff is simply a really accessible hobby, you can get into making art without any fancy equipment, and it can be a good avenue towards meeting people and making friends.

I'm by no means a 'good artist'. I've never had any formal training in any specific technical artistic skills, and I have no great natural talent by any means. But, my attitude is one inclined towards giving things a try. The way I see it - at the very least, picking up a brush and some bargain bin acrylic paints and slapping them onto some sort of surface is really intuitive and easy. Surely, if you know what painting, drawing, sculpting, papier-mâché, and so on is - then you know how to do it, at least at a basic level.

Armed with that sort of mindset and a dilettante's interest in the creative process - whenever I needed money in my early-twenties, I simply got thinking about how I could make stuff with my admittedly minimal skills and resources that could sell, and how best to sell it. I'd say, that attitude is ultimately one that carried me well. With it, I found a lot of basic practices that work to make your art-making profitable. Those practices are exactly what I hope to outline as best I can right here.

I've found that, when it comes to making art to sell, you'll ideally be looking to make something you can produce a lot of in one sitting. Small pieces that you can produce fairly rapidly once you've got a work-flow down will always be winners. I've personally had a lot of success doing tiny paintings on two-by-two inch canvases and flat river stones. You can learn to make earrings, badges, and pins pretty easily and cheaply. Dried flower arrangements and mushroom spore-prints are almost no-cost to do. Polymer clay charms, buttons, and trinkets are always popular. You could try to pick up rock-knapping, I find spearheads and arrowheads are big earners.

The things you should be focused on making definitely aren't 'high art' by any definition. They're tchotchkes at best. Little decorative knick-knacks and such. Kitschy stuff people just like. And your number one skill - if you want to make money "making art" - is knowing how to churn that sort of stuff out, at a decent rate, in your own home, at minimal expense.

For instance, let's say you wanted to get started selling tiny kitschy paintings as I did. Here's the exact process I followed, starting with just about twenty dollars for resources at the outset.

You take your twenty dollars to an art supply store. I'm sure you'll have one near-enough if you're in even a modestly-sized town - but if, for some reason, you can't find an art supply store nearby, adapt. I'm sure you know how to order stuff online.

With your twenty dollars, first thing you get a few bottles of cheap acrylic paint. This is paint for children, essentially. This is what you got in second grade art class. It's water-soluable, it's non-toxic. It's nothing special and it comes in a variety of simple colours. A bottle like this will be about a dollar a piece. I'd recommend you get a basic spectrum of them: red, yellow, orange, blue, green, purple, white and black.

You're also gonna want to pickup a basic paintbrush set while you're there. That will probably be a couple more dollars - and what you're really after is just a beginner set that includes a roughly one-inch wide, as well as small one that comes to a fine point.

Flanz dandelions growing among cultivated periwinkles.

That's half your twenty dollars gone. The other half you're gonna want to spend on as many tiny, ready-to-paint, mini-canvases as you can find. These are essentially just sturdy little squares with a rigid backing. They're are available from a lot of brands, and they usually come in packs of three or five. You usually see these for a dollar a pack. But, in my experience, these are often discounted if you get a lot of them or they're in the bargain bin half the time. So, you get as many of these as you can - and when you're at the art store checkout - you ask whoever's working the register if they'll be able to get more in in the future. Because if you stick with this, you are going to want lots and lots of these eventually.

Flanz dandelions growing among cultivated periwinkles.

Go home with your new purchases and set up a working space. Lay out all your little canvases on a table, or a desk, or the floor if you want. Lay down some old newspaper or something if you're worried you're gonna get paint everywhere. Get your brushes out, fill a jar with some water and get a cleaning rag ready.

Let's say you've got fifty of these little canvases - on all of them, put one small, roughly pea-sized drop of your white acrylic paint on them. Then, a second drop of one of the other colours. Let's say you do ten with a drop of red, ten with a drop of blue, ten with a drop of green, ten with purple, and ten with orange.

You use your biggest brush to mix the red and white together right on the canvas, that will give you just enough to give the whole square a coat of pink. You do that will all the red and white ones, now you've got 10 pink canvas squares. Rinse your brush off in the jar of water, wipe it dry on the rag. Then you do the same with the white and blue, now you've got 10 light blue canvases. Repeat until you've covered all your canvases in their respective mixed colours.

Now, let them all dry. This cheap acrylic stuff doesn't take long to dry. And on a small area, in one thin layer - it will probably only take about fifteen minutes maximum. Once they're dry - you've now got, say, fifty prepared canvases. What I did when I was developing this process for myself - I would go on websites like Twitter or Tumblr, and I would find these pithy, sometimes corny, one-liner jokes and phrases and slogans. I'd write out a big list of them, and start copying them onto the painted canvases with a fine-tipped paintbrush.

Go to Google Images right now, type in something like “tumblr funny inspirational post”. It's all trite garbage, sure. But it's exceedingly popular. People like this sort of stuff. Take some of those and paint the words onto your canvases.

Use your own discretion choosing what colours to use. I'd also usually go around the edges of the canvas a bit, or I'd add a simple little graphical detail. If you've got a design that works, make five, ten, twenty copies of it if you want. But make sure you've got a bit of variety. You make do dozens and dozens of these in an hour. You can make hundreds in an afternoon once you've got a handle on the process.

Flanz dandelions growing among cultivated periwinkles.

Once you've made all your stock. The next part of the process is the actual task of selling it effectively.

What I recommend - again, from a position of personal experience with what works and what doesn't: pack up all your finished artworks in a nice box. Get a second container, like a big empty coffee tin, remove the label, rinse it out, and make a slot in the top - you'll want something like that to put your money in soon.

Ideally too, it helps to have something like a folding table for this next step - but it can be managed without one in a pinch.

Once you've got all your paintings done up, the next day, you need somewhere to set up shop. Ideal places to do this would be anywhere like farmer's markets, village fetes, or ask the organisers of events like indie music gigs or poetry recitals if you can set up a merch table of your own. Alternatively, ask if you can hawk your paintings at trendy, artsy, young adult hangout spots like coffee klatches, indie bookstores, hipster bars - that sort of thing. But, even if you can find anywhere like that - you can go to somewhere like the local park, the concourse of your local shopping plaza area, or the boardwalk along the beach if you've got one nearby.

What I've found works when it comes to selling art made this way is that you simply set up a table somewhere people go past, and have these paintings out for people to see. I always tried to have one or two of each of design on the table at any given time, and the rest of the surplus in their box. You will immediately attract people who will come over to see what you're doing.

When it comes to actually moving merchandise, I maintain this is the most crucial step of the process. When people inquire as to the price of what you're selling, you let them know - it is entirely pay-what-you-want.

Trust me on this. If you tell people something like a two-inch canvas with a corny quote on it costs, say, five dollars- they will not sell at all. But when it's pay-what-you-want, seemingly every passerby happily gives you at least five dollars, and often more, for one they like. I can't say why that is. But it's true.

Whenever I did this, I'd typically sell out my entire day' stock in about an hour. And go home to empty out the coffee tin I let people put their banknotes in. You'll see mostly fives and tens, a lot of twenties, - I've inexplicably found fifties in there. It just works. And it's all cash-in-hand, same-day payment.

Now, if you're smart, at this point you take all your earnings, go back to the art store, buy as many tiny cavases as they've got and maybe some bigger bottles of cheap paint so you can get into this for the long-haul. Go back to Google, get more designs, repeat the process, start selling at new spot the next day, rinse and repeat.

One person can do this on their own, at a scale that can draw in potentially a thousand dollars a day easily - if they properly commit to it.
